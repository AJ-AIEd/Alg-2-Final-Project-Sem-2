Case File

Bogotá’s Chingaza Water System

In April 2024, Bogotá began water restrictions as Chingaza fell near crisis levels. That short window looked catastrophic. The longer system story was harder, and more mathematical.

Overview Investigation Arc

The model changed when the frame changed.

Do not start with the final graph. Experience why earlier models became incomplete.

Step 1

The system looks catastrophic.

A short April 2024 window made continued decline feel mathematically reasonable.

Sequence thinking: local trend, repeated change, extrapolation.

What would you predict from this graph?
Step 2

Zooming out revealed the missing story.

Across decades, the system shows fluctuation, drought cycles, recovery, infrastructure change, and intervention.

Historical frame: the same data can tell different stories.

Which timeframe is responsible?
Step 3

Recovery did not remove long-term pressure.

Population, consumption, and urban demand can compound even after reservoirs recover.

Exponential model: D(t) = 100(1 + r)t.

What compounds over time?
Step 4

Some growth becomes physically impossible.

Reservoirs, storage, treatment, transport, and environmental recovery have capacity limits and realistic domains.

Polynomial thinking: extrema, turning points, end behavior, constraints.

Where does the graph stop making physical sense?
Step 5

The question changed.

The investigation is no longer “Will water run out tomorrow?” It becomes: “When does pressure exceed capacity, and which assumptions make that prediction fragile?”

Synthesis: compare incomplete explanations inside one evolving system.

Short window: danger looks inevitable.

Historical frame: fluctuation and recovery appear.

Demand model: future pressure compounds.

Physical limits: capacity and domains matter.

Final insight: no single equation fully explains the system.

Sources used for the case anchors: Bogotá April 2024 reservoir reports, Bogotá June 2024 report, Bogotá April 2025 report, Chingaza supply context, and DANE population projection methodology.

Strong Focus Test

A strong investigation usually includes one main changing quantity, one measurable relationship, one prediction or threshold question, one physical or cost constraint, and one clear connection across mathematical lenses.

Class Roadmap

Five phases, one investigation

Roadmap = conceptual flow. Open a phase when you need the next modeling move.

Class 1

Entering the System

What relationships are we beginning to notice?

Conceptual move: Notice relationships before choosing equations.

Modeling question: Which variables seem connected, and which are hard to measure?

Thinking shift: From topic choice to a measurable system relationship.

Focus: Observation, variables, systems thinking, uncertainty.

Vlog: Discuss patterns, unclear ideas, assumptions, and important variables.

Class 2

Modeling Change

How is the system changing over time?

Conceptual move: Represent repeated change with terms, formulas, and accumulated totals.

Modeling question: Is change happening in steps, continuously, additively, or multiplicatively?

Thinking shift: From noticing change to predicting and testing reliability.

Focus: Sequences, series, prediction, recalculation.

Vlog: Explain why the model fits and where it begins to fail.

Formative Checkpoint 1 · End of Class 2

System Plan + Model Connection Check-In

Do the investigation pieces connect into a coherent system story?

Purpose: Confirm the system, variables, data, early sequence work, and planned models connect before deeper modeling begins.

Students show: chosen system, key variables, changing relationships, planned models, data sources, assumptions, and what each model may reveal or miss.

Teacher feedback focus: Are variables meaningful? Is the context measurable? Are models appropriate? Is the investigation too broad, too shallow, or forcing functions unnaturally?

Assessment role: Low-stakes formative check-in focused on coherence, redirection, and preventing disconnected Algebra 2 artifacts.

Class 3

Acceleration, Decay, and Thresholds

When does repeated change become accelerated or compounding?

Conceptual move: Model compounding, decay, and threshold moments.

Modeling question: What changes when growth is treated as continuous rather than sampled in steps?

Thinking shift: From prediction to threshold interpretation and model breakdown.

Focus: Exponential models, logarithmic solving, realism.

Vlog: Explain what the graph reveals and when exponential behavior stops making sense.

Formative Checkpoint 2 · Beginning of Class 4

Polynomial + Physical Constraints Check-In

Are physical constraints becoming visible mathematically?

Purpose: Confirm students understand why the polynomial model exists before final synthesis begins.

Students show: blueprint or sketch, dimensions, polynomial model, graph, annotations, early interpretation, and domain reasoning.

Students explain: why the relationship is polynomial, what suggests cubic behavior, what turning points mean physically, and which regions are unrealistic.

Teacher feedback focus: Long division meaning, factor interpretation, quadratic vs cubic behavior, end behavior, constraints, realism, and model comparison.

Class 4

Constraints, Capacity, and Cost

How do physical and economic constraints reshape the system?

Conceptual move: Use structure and graph behavior to understand constraints.

Modeling question: Which parts of the polynomial are physically meaningful, and which must be rejected?

Thinking shift: From “bigger is better” to tradeoffs, extrema, and restricted domains.

Focus: Polynomial structure, capacity, cost, physical interpretation.

Vlog: Explain how the cubic structure reveals constraints and impossible regions.

Class 5

Revision, Synthesis, and Communication

What story does our mathematics tell?

Conceptual move: Turn separate models into one mathematical claim.

Modeling question: What did each model reveal, and where did each stop being useful?

Thinking shift: From completed work to a defensible explanation of uncertainty.

Focus: Synthesis, revision, interpretation, limitations.

Vlog: Discuss how understanding evolved and what limitations remain.

Mathematical Lenses

Different structures reveal different parts of the system.

Use each lens to make a different kind of mathematical meaning. The goal is not to complete units in order; the goal is to understand the system more clearly.

Sequences & Series

Big question: How is the system changing over time?

Use this lens for repeated change, terms, totals, prediction, recalculation, and cumulative effects.

Exponentials & Logarithms

Big question: When does repeated change become accelerated, compounding, decaying, or threshold-based?

Use this lens for growth factors, thresholds, logarithmic solving, and model breakdown.

Polynomials

Big question: How do physical and economic constraints reshape the system?

Use this lens for structure, capacity, cost, extrema, end behavior, and realistic domains.

Required Mathematical Comparisons

Use comparisons only when they clarify the model.

Comparisons should reveal representation, structure, realism, interpretation, assumptions, or constraints.

Class 2 — Sequences & SeriesDiscrete change and accumulated change
  • Discrete vs continuous: Are values measured in steps, or can they change between measurements?
  • Arithmetic vs geometric: Is change added or multiplied each step?
  • Recursive vs explicit: Do you need the previous term, or can you jump directly to any term?
  • Term vs total: Does the model describe one moment or accumulated change?
Purpose: decide whether a sequence is the most honest representation of the system.
Class 3 — Exponentials & LogsGrowth, thresholds, and representation
  • Linear vs exponential: Is change constant, or does it depend on the current amount?
  • Sampled vs continuous: How does modeling continuously change the meaning compared to yearly terms?
  • Table vs graph: What does the graph reveal that the sequence table does not?
  • Thresholds: Why are logarithms needed to solve for an unknown time?
  • Realism: When does continuous exponential behavior stop making sense?
Purpose: connect representation choice to threshold meaning and prediction limits.
Class 4 — PolynomialsStructure, behavior, and physical constraints
  • Cubic structure: What does multiplying interacting dimensions reveal?
  • Factored vs standard form: Which form makes zeros, dimensions, or comparison easier to see?
  • Odd vs even degree: How would the graph behave differently if the degree changed?
  • Leading coefficient: Why does one side rise while the other falls?
  • End behavior: Interpret as x → ∞ and as x → −∞ in context.
  • Physical meaning: What do multiplicity, extrema, and restricted domains suggest?
Purpose: use graph structure to identify capacity, tradeoffs, and impossible regions.

Journal Philosophy

The journal is where your investigation becomes visible: uncertainty, revision, recalculation, critique, graph interpretation, changing assumptions, and mathematical reasoning all belong here.

The physical journal captures the group’s evolving mathematical thinking. Your individual digital evidence captures your own mathematical reasoning inside the group investigation.

Use this website page as the guide for what strong evidence looks like. Use the printable PDF as the place where the investigation is actively built.

Crossed-out thinking belongs here. Strong investigations often become more nuanced, not more certain.

The strongest rubric scores come from journals that make reasoning, revision, assumptions, interpretation, and individual thinking visible.

Visible Individual Thinking

The journal is shared, but grading is individual. Every student must contribute visible individual mathematical evidence inside the collaborative investigation.

Individual evidence may include graph annotations, recalculations, model critiques, threshold interpretations, comparison notes, AI critique reflections, synthesis explanations, revised assumptions, scatterplot interpretation, or limitation analysis.

The physical group journal is a shared thinking space. Individual digital evidence is the main place to show your own reasoning, interpretations, graph notes, AI critiques, reflections, and explanations.

Do not divide the mathematics by topic. This is not one student doing sequences, another doing exponentials, and another doing polynomials.

Every student must engage with sequences, exponentials, polynomials, interpretation, and revision.

Individual thinking must remain visible inside collaborative work.

Revision is evidence of stronger understanding

Students are expected to revise assumptions, update graphs, change models, critique unrealistic predictions, recalculate when assumptions change, and explain why their thinking evolved.

The strongest journals often contain arrows, annotations, crossed-out models, recalculations, competing ideas, and revised interpretations. Strong investigations often become more nuanced, not more certain.

Messy thinking is acceptable. Hidden thinking is not.

Reflection Checkpoints

Reflection Vlogs

Visible mathematical thinking in progress.

These are not polished presentations or cinematic productions. They are short mathematical conversations, reflection checkpoints, revision records, and visible evidence of evolving reasoning.

The goal is not performance. The goal is to make interpretation, critique, uncertainty, revision, and mathematical discussion visible.

Research on project-based learning emphasizes that reflection, critique, and revision should happen throughout a project, not only at the end. AVID Open Access

Structure

Upload one group vlog after each class phase: Class 1, Class 2, Class 3, Class 4, and the final synthesis after Class 5.

Think of each vlog as an investigation checkpoint, not a final performance.

Length

Recommended length: 3–5 minutes.

Keep the conversation focused, mathematical, reflective, and natural.

Strong Moments

Show revised assumptions, graph discussions, uncertainty, model comparisons, threshold interpretation, realism critique, and explanations of what changed.

What to Avoid

Avoid reading scripts, overediting, cinematic intros, effects, memorized speeches, or mechanically dividing roles by topic.

Make individual thinking visible

Every student should contribute mathematical reasoning in each vlog. Strong contributions include explaining a graph, critiquing an assumption, comparing models, discussing revision, interpreting context, identifying limitations, or defending a modeling decision.

Use a journal-centered format

The strongest vlogs are usually conversational, journal-centered, and annotation-driven. Point to journal pages, annotate graphs live, explain crossed-out thinking, compare old and new models, and show evolving reasoning visually.

  • What does this graph reveal that the equation alone does not?
  • What hidden variables might matter?
  • Where does the model stop making sense?
  • What revision made your investigation stronger?

AI + Model Fit

AI should deepen thinking, not replace thinking.

This investigation treats AI as a cognitive partner for critique, revision, interpretation, questioning assumptions, and identifying blind spots — not as a replacement for mathematical reasoning.

CNG SAIL Level L5 — Co-Create

Students remain responsible for understanding their mathematics, defending their choices, explaining assumptions, and interpreting limitations.

Reasoning Still Comes First

Strong mathematical thinking still requires graph ↔ algebra ↔ context ↔ interpretation.

How AI fits into the investigation

Use AI When

  • your investigation becomes uncertain,
  • your assumptions need critique,
  • your model stops making sense,
  • your interpretation feels incomplete,
  • or your reasoning needs revision.

Useful AI Support

  • comparing models,
  • identifying hidden variables,
  • questioning unrealistic assumptions,
  • interpreting graph behavior,
  • organizing thinking,
  • explaining r and R²,
  • checking recalculations,
  • or helping clarify communication.

Do Not Use AI To

  • replace your reasoning,
  • generate unexplained mathematics,
  • complete the investigation for you,
  • or hide meaningful AI use.

Flint AI: Cognitive Partner

Use Flint as a Socratic support tool for this investigation.

Flint is designed to challenge assumptions, deepen interpretation, compare models, support revision, and push mathematical reasoning through questioning.

Flint may provide more direct support for scatterplots, trendlines, Desmos setup, graph interpretation, r and R², and technical modeling tools.

Flint should function as: a cognitive partner, a reflective coach, and an inquiry support system. It is not an answer machine, a shortcut, or a replacement for understanding.

Launch Flint AI

Lightweight Model Fit Check

Correlation, r, and R²: How strong is the relationship?

This is a credibility and critique tool, not a full statistics unit. The goal is interpretation, not manual calculation.

Correlation does not prove causation. A high R² can still produce misleading conclusions if assumptions are weak, variables are missing, the context is misunderstood, or the relationship is interpreted carelessly.
What r and R² help reveal

r

Direction and strength of a linear relationship.

R²

How much variation is explained by the model.

Useful Questions

  • Is the relationship strong or weak?
  • What hidden variables might exist?
  • What still needs verification?
  • Does the model oversimplify reality?
  • Would a different model fit better?
  • Does this relationship imply causation? Why or why not?
Student task and required AI critique prompt

Student Task

Choose two variables from your investigation. Create a scatterplot, a trendline or model, and a short interpretation.

Then interpret r and/or R², critique the model’s limitations, identify hidden variables, and explain whether the relationship is actually meaningful.

If possible, verify using Desmos, Google Sheets, a graphing calculator, or another reliable tool. If verification is limited, state that honestly.

Required AI Critique Prompt

Ask AI: “What does r suggest about direction and strength? What does R² suggest about model fit? What limitations should we be careful about? What hidden variables might affect this relationship? Does this imply causation? Why or why not?”

Then reflect: What did AI help us notice? What still needs verification? What assumptions remain? How credible is this model? Where might this interpretation fail?

Final Reminder

AI can support mathematical thinking. It cannot replace interpretation, critique, revision, contextual reasoning, or intellectual responsibility.

A model is useful, but incomplete.
